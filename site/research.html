Research

Inference in social interaction

I study how people infer what others know, want, and attend to—and how these inferences can be modelled in humans and artificial systems.

Human social cognition · preprint

Learning preferences through interaction

How can a seller learn what a buyer values without being told their preferences? I designed a repeated bargaining task in which 75 buyer–seller dyads negotiated offers defined by three attributes, such as a car’s speed, comfort, and safety.

Buyers’ choices, rejection times, and first fixations carried different information about their preferences. Sellers adapted later offers using these cues. A hierarchical model describes them as Bayesian learners balancing expected utility with information gain. Access to gaze changed how sellers used attention, without improving overall performance.

Computational cognition · preprint

Which human cues can language models use?

I evaluated Llama 3.1, Centaur, and GPT-5 as sellers in the bargaining task, supplying human choices and descriptions of response times and gaze. The models used gaze information to improve inference and offers, but exploited response times much less effectively than humans.

GPT-5 outperformed the two open models. Yet humans recovered the hidden preference weights more accurately, while models could turn imperfect estimates into competitive offers. The distinction matters: strong task performance does not establish human-like social inference.

Decision neuroscience · preprint

Where value becomes evidence

Why do anxiety and depression relate to biased choices? Across independent cohorts from the US and India, this project combined risky decisions, hierarchical drift-diffusion modelling, and EEG measures of evidence accumulation.

Higher symptom scores were associated with weaker sensitivity to value during evidence accumulation, while decision caution remained largely unchanged. Risk and loss aversion showed little association with symptoms. This points to the translation of value into evidence as a possible shared mechanism.

Social decision neuroscience · preprint

Predicting others without confusing them with ourselves

Participants first made risky choices for themselves, then learned and predicted the choices of simulated agents with similar or dissimilar risk preferences. EEG allowed us to ask when self–other distinction and valuation emerged within a decision.

An earlier signal distinguished predictions for a similar other from self decisions, followed by a later difficulty-related signal shared across decision types. Contrary to the original hypothesis, the dissimilar-other comparison did not show the same reliable early distinction.

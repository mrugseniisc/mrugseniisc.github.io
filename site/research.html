Research

Inference in social interaction

I study how people infer what others know, want, and attend to—and how these inferences can be modelled in humans and artificial systems.

Human social cognition

Learning preferences through interaction

In repeated bargaining, one person must learn another’s hidden preferences from a sequence of offers and decisions. I designed a multi-round, multi-attribute task with eye tracking and studied how sellers use buyers’ choices, response times, and gaze to adapt later offers.

The project combines behavioral experiments with a hierarchical Bayesian model of preference learning. The current preprint reports data from 75 buyer–seller dyads.

Computational cognition

Can AI infer preferences from behavior?

I use the bargaining task as a benchmark for large language models and recurrent neural networks. This work tests whether artificial agents can recover preferences from human choices, response times, and gaze, and whether they use process cues in ways that support effective interaction.

Decision neuroscience

From value to evidence, in people and models

My work also examines how anxiety and depression relate to the translation of value into decision evidence, and how people represent decisions made by similar and dissimilar others. These projects connect behavioral choice with computational models and neural measures.
